Getting documents into the system

Bring in the documents you already have. Add new ones as they are made. Every document has one clear home, one clear owner, and one clear approved version.

Part A: Bringing in what you already have

Most teams start with a spreadsheet or a shared drive full of files. The system reads your list first and shows you what needs fixing. Nothing is saved until you say go.

1

Load your list

Upload a spreadsheet. Each line is one version of one document.

Takes about 2 minutes
2

The system checks every line

It looks for wrong names, documents in the wrong folder, and duplicates. It also finds any missing versions.

3

You fix or skip what it flags

Names can be corrected in place. Folders can be changed with one click. Anything you do not want can be skipped.

4

Press start and watch it run

Documents arrive in small batches. You can pause at any time. If the page closes, reopen it and it carries on from the same line.

Missing versions are shown, never invented

If version 1 is missing from your old list but version 2 is there, the system marks version 1 as “Not on file”. It will not guess. You can upload the real file into that gap later.

Part B: Adding a new document

A new document follows the same path every time. Two different people sign it off before it becomes the approved version.

1

Choose the file and say what it is

Pick the file. Give it a short title and a reason for adding it.

2

Pick a checker and an approver

The checker reads it. The approver signs it off. They must be two different people, and neither can be you.

3

Write a one-line summary

Say what the file is and why it exists. This line stays with the document for good.

4

Send it

The checker is told. Once they check it and the approver signs, it becomes the approved version.

You cannot approve your own work

The system will not let the person who added a file also check or approve it. This keeps every document honest, and it is the first thing auditors look for.

Keeping track of changes

Documents change. Revisions keep a record of every change. Short summary lines tell anyone, in seconds, what changed and why.

What is a revision?

A revision is one version of a document. Think of it like saving a new copy of a file, except each copy has a number, a reason, and a sign-off. Nothing is written over. Old copies stay on record.

Current

The approved version. This is the one people should use. Only one revision is current at a time.

Latest

The newest revision, even if nobody has approved it yet. It may not be ready to use.

Try it: a revision history

Click any revision to read its summary line.

    U100-PID-1001
    Pump layout, Unit 100
    Current: Rev 1

    Summary lines: why they matter

    Every revision needs one short line. It answers two questions: what changed, and why. Here are examples from real work.

    Rev 0 · Pump layout issued for reviewFirst issue for internal review after the layout meeting.Added by J. Okafor · 3 Feb
    Rev 1 · Moved pump 300 mm north after site surveySurvey found the foundation pad was offset. Layout updated to match.Added by A. Rahman · 10 Apr
    Rev 2 · Added bypass valve on the cooling lineHazard review asked for a bypass. Waiting for check.Added by M. Chen · 28 Oct

    Rule of thumb

    If someone can read just the summary line and understand the change, the line is good enough.

    Who does what

    Four kinds of people use the system. Most people are only ever in one or two of these roles.

    Adds a revision

    1. Chooses the file.
    2. Writes the summary line and the reason.
    3. Names a checker and an approver.

    Checks a revision

    1. Opens the file and reads the summary.
    2. Checks it is right.
    3. Passes it on, or returns it with a reason.

    Approves a revision

    1. Reads the check.
    2. Signs off, or returns it with a reason.
    3. A signed revision becomes Current.

    Everyone else

    1. Searches for the document.
    2. Reads the Current version.
    3. Reads the history and summaries.
